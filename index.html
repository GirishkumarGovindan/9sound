NCERT Activity 10.1 — Procedure & Observation Checkpoints
  1. Stretch a rubber band across an open cardboard box and pluck it: sound is heard only while the band vibrates; once stopped, sound ceases immediately.
  2. Increase tension (stretching tighter) or switch to a thin rubber band: it vibrates faster (higher frequency, shorter time period), producing a shriller high-pitched sound.
  3. Remove the box and pluck between two fingers: sound is still produced, but it is noticeably fainter without the hollow box acting as an acoustic resonator.
Key Physics Conclusion: Sound is produced by periodic to-and-fro motion (vibrations). Pitch increases with vibration frequency (ν = 1/T), while a resonating air cavity increases loudness.

Why Use a Soft Rubber Pad?
• Striking on a soft rubber pad sets the steel prongs into pure, gentle oscillation without damaging the metal tines or creating harsh metallic overtones.
• Touching water transfers mechanical kinetic energy from the invisible vibrating tines into visible circular surface waves!
NCERT Activity 10.2 — Procedure & Observation Checkpoints
  1. Hold the tuning fork by its stem, strike one prong gently on a soft rubber pad, and bring it near your ear: a pure single-frequency hum is heard.
  2. Rotate the vibrating prongs through different orientations (0°–360°): sound is heard in all directions, showing sound radiates outward in 3D.
  3. Touch a vibrating prong gently to a water surface: concentric ripples and tiny splashes appear immediately, proving the prongs are oscillating rapidly.
Key Physics Conclusion: Even when mechanical vibrations are too fast and tiny for the eye to resolve directly, touching water makes the oscillatory motion unmistakably visible.

Speed Comparison (Table 10.1)
• Sound travels ~11× faster in Wood and ~15× faster in Steel (5000 m/s) than in Air (340 m/s) because tightly packed solid atoms pass vibrations rapidly with minimal spreading loss!
NCERT Activity 10.3 — Procedure & Observation Checkpoints
  1. Stand at opposite ends of a desk and have a friend gently knock or scratch it while your ear is in the air: the sound is faint.
  2. Press one ear firmly against the desk surface and cover the other ear: the exact same gentle knock sounds much louder and clearer!
  3. Try the 340 m Steel Fence preset (Pause & Ponder 11): sound arrives through steel in 0.068 s and through air in 1.000 s (Δt = 0.932 s > 0.1 s, heard twice!).
Key Physics Conclusion: Sound propagates readily through solids, and travels much faster with less energy attenuation than through gases.

Why Spacewalks Are Silent (Section 10.2.1)
• Sound is a mechanical wave that requires material particles (solid, liquid, or gas) to collide and pass compressions forward.
• In a vacuum (outer space), there are no particles to vibrate—so sound cannot propagate!
NCERT Activity 10.4 — Procedure & Observation Checkpoints
  1. Tap two metal spoons in air (Fig. 10.6a), then submerge both spoons inside a tub of water without touching the walls and tap again (Fig. 10.6b).
  2. You clearly hear the metallic clink: vibrations travel through water (1500 m/s), cross into air (340 m/s), and reach your ear.
  3. Switch to the Vacuum Bell Jar (Fig. 10.7) and pump out the air: even though the hammer still visibly strikes the bell, the sound fades to complete silence!
Key Physics Conclusion: Sound travels through solids, liquids, and gases, but cannot travel through a vacuum.

Longitudinal Wave Anatomy
• Compression (C): Coils/air molecules crowded together → High density (Graph Crest).
• Rarefaction (R): Coils/air molecules spread apart → Low density (Graph Trough).
• Particles oscillate back & forth parallel to propagation—they do NOT travel with the wave!
NCERT Activity 10.5 — Procedure & Observation Checkpoints
  1. Mark one turn of a stretched horizontal slinky with a red marker and give the end a push-and-pull disturbance.
  2. Observe how regions of crowded turns (Compressions) and spread-out turns (Rarefactions) travel down the slinky, while the red marker only oscillates back and forth around its rest position.
  3. Compare the slinky turns directly with the air-filled piston tube (Fig. 10.9) and the Density–Distance sine curve (Fig. 10.16) below it.
Key Physics Conclusion: Sound waves are longitudinal mechanical waves: medium particles vibrate parallel to the direction of wave propagation, transferring energy without net transport of matter.

NCERT Activity 10.6 — Procedure & Observation Checkpoints
  1. Stretch a rubber/cellophane sheet tightly over a wide-mouthed bowl and sprinkle lightweight grains evenly across it.
  2. Strike a nearby metal plate with a wooden beater without touching the bowl: sound waves travel through air, set the membrane vibrating, and make the grains jump!
  3. Strike harder (larger amplitude): air particles undergo larger displacements, transferring more energy so the grains jump noticeably higher.
Key Physics Conclusion: Sound is a form of mechanical energy. Waves with larger amplitude carry more energy (E ∝ A²) and have higher intensity.

  1. Play or sing the ascending musical notes Sa, Re, Ga, Ma, Pa, Dha, Ni, Sa' while observing the Phyphox-style Audio Spectrum analyzer.

Wave Classification & Real-World Applications
• Infrasonic (< 20 Hz): Earthquakes, storms, elephants.
• Audible (20 Hz – 20 kHz): Human speech & music (λ = 17.2 m to 1.72 cm).
• Ultrasonic (> 20 kHz): Bat echolocation, SONAR, medical ultrasonography.
NCERT Activity 10.8 — Procedure & Observation Checkpoints
  1. Start at 100 Hz and increase in steps of 100 Hz up to 1000 Hz: the sound becomes progressively shriller (higher pitch) as wavelength shortens.
  2. Set to 50 Hz and reduce below 20 Hz: once below 20 Hz (infrasonic), the human ear can no longer hear the sound even though the speaker cone still oscillates!
  3. Test above 20,000 Hz (20 kHz): ultrasonic waves are inaudible to humans, yet bats, dolphins, and SONAR systems use them for echolocation.
Key Physics Conclusion: The human audible range spans 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz (20 kHz). Speed in air remains constant (~344 m/s at 22°C), so wavelength varies inversely with frequency (λ = v / ν).
